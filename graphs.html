🕸️ Graphs

Things (nodes) and the connections between them (edges). When "who is connected to whom" is the whole point, you need a graph.

Everyday picture: a road map. Cities are the dots, roads are the lines. Or social media: people are nodes, friendships are edges. Unlike a tree, a graph can have loops and there is no single "top".

How it works

  1. Nodes and edges. A node can be anything: a city, a person, a web page. An edge says "these two are connected".
  2. BFS (breadth-first search) explores all neighbours first, then their neighbours — like ripples in a pond. It uses a queue, and finds the route with the fewest hops.
  3. DFS (depth-first search) follows one road as far as it goes, then backs up and tries another — like exploring a maze. It uses a stack.
  4. Remember where you've been, or you'll walk in circles forever on a looped map.

Real life

🗺️ Maps & GPS
Finding a route is searching a graph where roads have lengths.
👥 Social networks
"People you may know" = friends of friends = BFS two hops out.
🔗 The web
Pages are nodes, links are edges. Search engines crawl this graph.
📦 Dependencies
Package managers track what depends on what — a graph.

Space: V = nodes, E = edges. An adjacency list (each node keeps a list of its neighbours) costs O(V + E) — great for sparse graphs like road maps, where each city links to only a few others. An adjacency matrix (a V × V grid) costs O(V²) however few edges exist, but answers "are these two connected?" instantly. BFS and DFS also need O(V) extra memory to remember visited nodes plus the queue or stack.

Use it when the connections between things are the point: routes, networks, dependencies, "who knows whom".
Avoid it when your data has no meaningful connections — a plain array or table is simpler. For huge, sparse graphs avoid the adjacency matrix: its O(V²) memory is mostly empty.
