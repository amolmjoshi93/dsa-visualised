⚡ Quick Sort

Pick one item as the pivot. Move everything smaller to its left and everything bigger to its right. The pivot is now in its final spot — then do the same to each side.

Everyday picture: organising a crowd by height. Pick one person as the reference. Everyone shorter stands on their left, everyone taller on their right. Now you have two smaller crowds — repeat for each. Soon every group has one person, and the whole crowd is in order.

Watch it

The purple bar is the pivot. After each partition, the pivot turns green: it's exactly where it belongs forever. Try Reversed or Nearly sorted to see why this simple "last item as pivot" version struggles on them.

How it works

  1. Choose a pivot (here, the last item in the section).
  2. Partition: scan the section; move values smaller than the pivot to the left part.
  3. Place the pivot right after the "smaller" group. It's now in its final position.
  4. Recurse: do the same on the left part and on the right part.

If the pivot splits things roughly in half each time, we get about log n levels of n work: n log n. A bad pivot (like always the smallest) gives lopsided splits and n². Real libraries choose pivots cleverly (e.g. median of three, or random) to avoid this.

Real life

📚 Standard libraries
C's qsort and C++'s std::sort (introsort) are quick-sort based.
🔬 Big in-memory data
It sorts in place and is very cache-friendly, which makes it fast in practice.
🥇 "Top-k" problems
A cousin of quick sort (quickselect) finds the median or the 10 best items without fully sorting.
