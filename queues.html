🎟️ Queues

A line where newcomers join at the back and the person at the front is served first. Fair, simple, predictable.

Everyday picture: the queue at a railway ticket counter. New people stand at the back. The counter serves whoever has been waiting longest — the front. This rule is called FIFO: First In, First Out. (A stack is the opposite: LIFO.)

How it works

  1. Enqueue — add to the back of the line.
  2. Dequeue — remove from the front. That's the one who waited the longest.
  3. Both are instant, because we keep track of both ends and never need to search the middle.

Real life

🖨️ Print jobs
Documents print in the order they were sent.
📨 Message systems
Emails, SMS, and background jobs wait in queues until a worker picks them up.
🎮 Matchmaking
Players wait in a lobby queue; whoever's been waiting longest is matched first.
🚑 Priority queues
A variation: an emergency room serves the most urgent patient first, not the earliest.

Time & space complexity

n = number of items. Best / average / worst describe how lucky or unlucky the input is.

OperationBestAverageWorst
Enqueue (join the back)O(1)O(1)O(1)
Dequeue (leave the front)O(1)O(1)O(1) (O(n) if built carelessly on a plain array)
Peek at the frontO(1)O(1)O(1)
Search for a valueO(1)O(n)O(n)
Space (storing n items)O(n)O(n)O(n)

Space: one slot per waiting item, so O(n). Tip: removing the first item of a plain array shifts everything (O(n)); real queues use a linked list or a "circular buffer" so both ends stay O(1).

Use it when order of arrival matters and fairness is the goal. Queues also power breadth-first search on graphs.
Avoid it when urgency should beat arrival order (use a priority queue) or you need to reach into the middle. And don't build one by removing from the front of a plain array on big data — that shifts every item, O(n). Use a linked list or a circular buffer.
