📦 Arrays

The simplest data structure: items stored side by side in numbered slots. Almost everything else is built on top of it.

Everyday picture: a row of numbered lockers in a school corridor. If I say "open locker 42", you walk straight to it — you don't check lockers 1 to 41 first. But if someone wants a new locker squeezed in at number 3, every locker after it has to shift one place along.

How it works

  1. Items sit next to each other in memory. The computer remembers where the first one lives.
  2. Position × size = address. To read item 5, it jumps straight to start + 5 slots. That is why reading is instant, however big the array is.
  3. Inserting or deleting in the middle means shifting. Everything after the change has to move over by one slot to keep the row gap-free.
  4. Finding a value you only know by content means looking through the boxes one by one (see linear search), unless the array is sorted (see binary search).

Real life

📷 Photo gallery
Photo number 7 is shown instantly because the app jumps to index 6 in a list of photos.
🎵 Playlist
"Play track 12" is an index lookup. Reordering a playlist shifts items.
🖼️ Pixels
A screen is one big array of colour values. Drawing is writing to positions.

Speed at a glance

OperationSpeedWhy
Read by positionO(1)Direct jump
Add at the endO(1)Usually just place it in the next slot
Insert / delete in the middleO(n)Shift everything after it
Search by value (unsorted)O(n)Check every item
Use it when you mostly read by position or add to the end, and the size is fairly predictable.
Avoid it when you constantly insert or remove at the front or middle of a big collection. Look at linked lists or hash tables instead.
